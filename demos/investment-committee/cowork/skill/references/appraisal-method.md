## Relevant method

### 1. Establish rationale and objectives

Define the case for change, a theory of change, the baseline or business-as-usual outcome, SMART objectives, and strategic fit. Objectives should describe the outcomes sought and provide the common basis for comparing options. State assumptions and evidence strength. `[Green Book 2026, ch. 2, paras. 2.7-2.11; ch. 4, paras. 4.1, 4.9-4.27]`

### 2. Generate a broad range of options

Avoid starting with a predetermined solution. Generate a proportionate but genuinely broad longlist, define critical success factors, and document why choices advance or fall away. Consider scope, solution, delivery, implementation, and funding, including staged designs where relevant. `[Green Book 2026, ch. 2, paras. 2.12-2.16; ch. 5, paras. 5.1-5.16, 5.21-5.35]`

### 3. Preserve a baseline and a do-minimum comparator

Business as usual provides the benchmark even when it does not meet objectives. A do-minimum option is the least-cost option that just achieves the objectives and helps expose unnecessary features. A shortlist should offer genuine choices, including less and more ambitious variants where proportionate. `[Green Book 2026, ch. 4, paras. 4.14-4.19; ch. 5, paras. 5.17-5.20, 5.35]`

### 4. Move from longlist to shortlist

Longlist appraisal tests options against objectives and critical success factors. Apart from the baseline comparator, options that cannot achieve the objectives should not proceed. Shortlist appraisal then examines the viable options in greater detail and may send an option back if new evidence undermines viability. `[Green Book 2026, ch. 5, paras. 5.9-5.20; ch. 6, paras. 6.1-6.4]`

### 5. Compare monetisable and unmonetisable effects

Identify material costs and benefits for each option. Classify effects as monetisable or unmonetisable; unmonetisable effects may be quantitative or qualitative. Focus effort on effects that distinguish the options, and do not omit important effects merely because they cannot be expressed in money. Keep financial impact distinct from wider value. `[Green Book 2026, ch. 2, paras. 2.17-2.28; ch. 6, paras. 6.15-6.31]`

### 6. Treat risk and uncertainty explicitly

Explain uncertain assumptions, scenario ranges, downside exposure, dependencies, reversibility, mitigations, transferred risks, and residual risks. Use sensitivity or scenario analysis proportionately. Flexibility and phasing can have value when decisions are difficult to reverse. `[Green Book 2026, ch. 6, paras. 6.60-6.77, 6.102-6.105]`

### 7. Address optimism bias without inventing values

Appraisal should recognise the systematic tendency to underestimate cost and duration and overestimate benefits. Adjustments should be evidence-based and reduced only as specific risks are understood and prevented. In this skill, do not create an optimism-bias percentage or recalculate supplied financials; report whether the supplied case addresses the issue. `[Green Book 2026, ch. 6, paras. 6.78-6.84]`

### 8. Make a balanced value-for-money judgement

Summary metrics are an initial input, not a decision rule. Judge performance against objectives together with monetisable and unmonetisable effects, financial impact, risk and uncertainty, and relevant distributional effects. Explain why the preferred option is stronger than alternatives. `[Green Book 2026, ch. 2, paras. 2.4-2.6, 2.29-2.32; ch. 6, paras. 6.93-6.105]`

### 9. Present results transparently

Present alternatives consistently, including option descriptions, quantitative results, unmonetisable effects, financial impact, and risks or uncertainties. Use an executive summary and make the reason for the preferred option explicit rather than reducing the result to one metric. `[Green Book 2026, ch. 9, paras. 9.1-9.6]`

### 10. Plan monitoring and evaluation before approval

Define how delivery and benefits will be monitored, how outcomes will be evaluated, what evidence will be collected, and how learning will inform adaptation. A robust evaluation plan should exist before final approval and should revisit objectives, realised costs and benefits, distributional effects, risks, and unintended consequences. `[Green Book 2026, ch. 10, paras. 10.1-10.10]`
